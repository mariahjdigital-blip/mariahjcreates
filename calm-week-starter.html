A note before we start

First, I see you

You’re the one who remembers the permission slip, the dentist, the snack, and the thing your kid said three weeks ago. You’re also the one with a goal that’s just yours, even if you haven’t said it out loud lately. Both are true, and neither makes you ungrateful or selfish.

I’ve been there, and I still find myself there often. You’re carrying about 27 tabs in your brain all day, and not one of them ever feels safe to close. I get it. But I didn’t want it to always feel like this, so I went looking for a simpler way to run my days, with fewer steps, so I could feel less chaotic and more calm.

A promise, so we start honest

This won’t make the hard days disappear. It will give you a floor, a rhythm, and a few kind scripts so the hard days stop running the show. Nothing here asks you to become a frantic version of yourself.

How to use it

Set it up once (about an hour, on a Sunday if you can). After that it’s about 15 minutes a week. Take what helps, change what doesn’t fit, and skip the rest.

xo, MJ

Part 2

The invisible list, then your Big 3

Step 1: The invisible list (10 minutes). Set a timer and write down everything you do in a normal week that keeps your family running: the remembering, the planning, the driving, the comforting, the “who needs what by Friday.” Don’t edit. Then read it back.

That’s a lot. You already do a lot, and you don’t have to prove it by doing more.

Step 2: Your Big 3. Now choose only three things that matter most this season.

For my family
For my own goals
For me

If something isn’t on your Big 3, it can be small, shared, delayed or dropped.

Part 3

Your floor and your ceiling

My floor

The minimum that makes a day count. On a hard day, hitting your floor is full credit.

My ceiling

What a great day looks like. It’s a bonus, not a standard.

Examples of a floor: everyone fed, one load of laundry, one kind word to myself.

15 minutes, once a week

Pick one 10-minute evening reset (clear counters, one basket, music on).

Part 5

Staying present when you’re stretched

Being present doesn’t mean being perfect or never getting frustrated. It means having a way to come back. Here are five small tools.

1

The 90-second pause

Stop. Take three slow breaths and drop your shoulders. Lower your voice before you speak, not after.

2

Name the tank

Tell your kids the truth in a few words: “I’m getting frustrated. I’m going to take a minute and then I’ll be back.” Then actually come back.

3

The repair

When you snap, repair it fast: “I was short with you earlier. That wasn’t about you, and I’m sorry. Can we try again?” Kids learn more from the repair than from a perfect day.

4

The after-school landing

For the first 20 minutes after pickup: a snack, no questions, one small connection (a hug, a joke, sitting next to them). Save the “how was your day” for later.

5

The kind no

For extra asks that don’t fit your Big 3: “Thank you for thinking of me. I can’t take that on right now.” You don’t owe an explanation.

If the heaviness doesn’t lift, please tell someone you trust or a professional. You’re not meant to carry it alone.
